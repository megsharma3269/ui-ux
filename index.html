UX Case Study · 2024

SHOT turns a 90-second coffee reorder into a 2-tap, 30-second habit.

A coffee-first quick-commerce concept for Dubai — designed around habit and time-of-day instead of restaurant discovery. Speculative redesign under the Noon Minutes umbrella.

Role: Product & UX Design Market: Dubai, UAE Format: Mobile-first native app Status: Speculative
01 — Context

What is SHOT?

SHOT is a concept for a coffee-first quick-commerce ordering experience under the Noon Minutes umbrella in Dubai. Think Zepto or Blinkit, but built specifically around coffee ordering behavior — not general food delivery.

Honesty note: This is a speculative redesign. I have no affiliation with Noon Minutes and did not collaborate with their product team. All research, personas, and design decisions are my own, based on desk research, competitive teardown, and my own experience of the Dubai coffee-delivery market.

The opportunity

The UAE food-delivery market was valued at $3.93B in 2023, projected to reach $11.18B by 2030. Four major players dominate Dubai — Talabat, Careem Food, Deliveroo, and Keeta. But every one of them treats coffee like just another food item.

02 — Research

Competitive landscape

Four dominant players, all built as restaurant-discovery marketplaces. None optimizes for coffee's unique behavior: habitual, highly customized, time-sensitive, low AOV, one-thumb.

PlayerPositioningKey StrengthKey Weakness
Talabat Incumbent, largest scale Personalization engine, 9-country footprint Poor failure recovery, cluttered discovery
Careem Food Super-app ecosystem Bundled wallet + rides + food, Careem Plus Tab-switching friction; food is one tab among many
Keeta Disruptor (Meituan-owned) Aggressive pricing, drone trials, "on-time promise" Too new — feature parity gaps expected
Deliveroo Premium curator Visual restraint, curation, strong photography Limited vendor selection vs Talabat

Key insights

1. Restaurant-first is wrong for coffee. All four apps make users search for a "restaurant" to find their drink. Coffee drinkers think in drinks, not restaurants.

2. Repeat orders are 60–80% of volume, but reordering is slow. Best-case: ~6 taps, 60+ seconds. Aisha needs it in 2 taps, under 30 seconds.

3. No app has time-of-day intelligence. The home screen at 8am looks identical to 3pm. Morning ordering (habitual, urgent) is completely different from afternoon (exploratory, treat-seeking).

05 — Problem

SHOT helps time-pressured Dubai coffee drinkers reorder their usual in under 30 seconds — by replacing restaurant-first food-delivery UX with a habit-first, coffee-native experience built around who they are and when they're ordering.

06 — Principles

Five rules every decision was tested against

01

Habit over discovery

"Your usual" is the app. Menu is secondary.

02

One thumb, no thinking

Every primary action reachable in the bottom third.

03

Coffee-native customization

Visual pickers, sliders, toggles. Never a form.

04

Time is the interface

The app looks different at 8am vs 3pm.

05

Confidence over choice

Fewer options, better decisions.

Variation C · Recipe

The layered stack

A glass fills segment by segment as you build. Bands on the right control each layer. The recipe is visible — you're stacking ingredients into a cup.

Which one to carry into hi-fi

Variation B (The Living Cup) is the strongest candidate. The live drink preview does emotional work — you see what you're ordering. Everything visible at once means the fastest possible customization. Each variable gets the input type suited to it.

Variation A is the strong fallback. Simpler to implement, more novel, but relies on reading — cognitive load Aisha doesn't have at 7:52am.

Variation C is the most memorable but riskiest. Stacked bands require more taps and lack the live cup preview. Better for a slower "explore" mode.

08 — Decisions & Trade-offs

Choices that shaped the system

Habit-first as architecture, not a feature

The "Your usual" card isn't a shortcut — it's the app's homepage. This is a structural commitment, not a widget bolted onto a discovery feed.

Time-of-day changes layout, not just copy

Morning and afternoon home screens use the same building blocks but re-choreograph them. This proves the principle isn't cosmetic — it's structural.

Three explorations before committing

The Drink Builder explored three parallel mental models (language, object, recipe) before choosing the Living Cup for the primary path. All three shown to demonstrate visible thinking.

Group orders are people-based, not group-based

Maria's meetings don't have a fixed group week-to-week. The stable thing is individual drink preferences. Memory lives at the person level.

Tracking is time-based, not geographic

Users care where in the process their coffee is, not where the rider is on Sheikh Zayed Road. This directly responds to Talabat's documented "premature delivered" trust problem.

The delivered screen is a retention moment

Primary action converts one order into a habit. The most valuable moment for retention is right after a good experience.

The menu is deliberately conventional

Not every screen should be experimental. Browsing is a job that rewards familiarity. Restraint is a decision.

09 — Reflection

What's next

What would I test first?

The sentence vs living-cup customization UI (Variation A vs B). Both have strong hypotheses. 3–5 usability tests with real Dubai coffee drinkers would settle it before committing to hi-fi polish.

What's intentionally out of scope?

No live user testing yet — personas are proto-personas from desk research. No settings, profile, or account screens. No detailed error/edge-case states. Focused on the core purchase loop.

What would change with a real client?

Real user interviews, one round of usability testing before hi-fi, coordination with backend/operational constraints, accessibility audit (WCAG contrast + screen reader flow).

What did I learn?

Product design isn't about making screens. It's about making decisions — and defending them with research. The strongest design choice here was structural (habit-first architecture), not visual.
